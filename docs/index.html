What is this?

What do opening and closing mean?

Today a receiver's openness is reported as one number, "separation": the yards between the receiver and the closest defender at the moment of the throw. But that gap is always moving. Window Trend looks at the half second before the throw and asks which way it is going.

Both drawings end with the same 2.5 yards of space, so a separation number calls them the same throw. Window Trend tells them apart.

The exact rule. We measure how fast the gap is changing over the half second before the throw. Growing by more than 1 yard per second is opening. Shrinking by more than 1 yard per second is closing. Anything in between is steady.

How to read it: when the receiver is tightly covered (1 to 2 yards of space), a pass into an opening window is caught 72 times in 100, against 42 in 100 when the window is closing. When he is already wide open (5 to 8 yards), it makes no difference.

Not shown: with more than 8 yards of space it is 85% against 80%.

  • More yards after the catch. On short throws (less than 5 yards downfield), the receiver gains 6.7 yards after the catch when the window is opening and 4.0 yards when it is closing. (711 and 973 caught passes)
  • Biggest against man coverage. Against Cover-1, where each defender follows one receiver, 80% of throws are caught when the window is opening and 53% when it is closing. (326 and 440 throws)
  • More expected points. A throw into an opening window is worth about 0.55 points to the offense on average; into a closing window, about 0.10. (Expected points: how much a play raises the score a team can expect from that drive.)

1,222 throws

About half of all throws to running backs arrive with a defender closing in. When the window is opening instead, their catches gain 9.3 yards after the catch, against 5.1.

  • 21.8%of throws: window opening
  • 48.4%of throws: window closing

Where the player lines up matters too.

  • Split out wide and tightly covered (1 to 3 yards of space): 72% caught when opening, 43% when closing. (195 and 422 throws)
  • Tight ends lined up next to the offensive line and tightly covered: 59% against 57%. No real difference, so the idea does not help there. (41 and 77 throws, a small sample)
  • Out of the backfield: a defender is closing on 49% of throws.

Positions are roster positions. The 57 throws to fullbacks are not in the running back card.

Who would use this?

  • A coachTo time throws so the ball leaves while the gap is still growing. Running backs are the clearest case: 48% of their throws arrive with a defender closing, and their catches on opening windows gain about 4 more yards after the catch. On defense, the team lookup hints at which coverages were closing on the receiver (small samples).
  • A scoutTo put a catch rate in context: did he catch more or fewer balls than the league does on throws with the same space and window? For quarterbacks it is different: how often a quarterback throws into an opening window carries over from one half of his games to the other, so it says more about the passer and the offense than about the receiver.
  • A broadcasterTo explain a replay with one color: blue if the receiver was pulling away at the throw, orange if the defender was closing. It helps explain why two throws with the same space ended differently.

What it cannot tell you

  • We never see the catch. The tracking data stops half a second after the throw. We know which way the gap was moving when the ball left, not how much space there was when it arrived.
  • It is not a grade for getting open. A receiver who got many opening windows in half of his games did not get more of them in the other half. The windows come from quarterback timing, play design and coverage.
  • The samples are small. This is eight weeks: 7,263 throws in total, 143 to 284 per team, and as few as 25 for a player. Team ranks and player numbers are a rough guide, not a verdict.
  • It shows a pattern, not a cause. Short passes, play design and the type of coverage all move the window too. Opening and closing are also a simple cut (1 yard per second), not a law of nature.
